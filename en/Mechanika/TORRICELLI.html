The Torricelli Experiment

Atmospheric Pressure

Experiments

In all three experiments, we observe that atmospheric pressure can easily balance out the hydrostatic pressure of a water column shorter than 10 m10\text{ m}. Thus, the force resulting from atmospheric pressure keeps the water inside the glass, as it effortlessly supports the weight of the water. This is the same reason why a balloon attached to the bottom of a water-filled tube does not bulge outward. Bulging inward is out of the question, since water is incompressible.

The balloon only bulges outward when the stopper at the top of the tube is removed. At that point, atmospheric pressure pushes on the water column from both above and below, and these forces cancel each other out. In this case, the weight of the water is balanced by the elastic force exerted by the balloon, so the balloon bulges outward.

The situation is similar for the water bottle. As long as the cap is sealed, the external atmospheric pressure maintains equilibrium with the internal pressure, and the water does not flow out. This remains true even if there is some air inside the system. In that case, a small amount of water flows out, but the air inside expands, causing its pressure to drop slightly. At the height of the hole, the pressure inside the water is exactly equal to the external atmospheric pressure, so the water does not flow out of the hole. The situation is completely different when the cap is removed. Then, the water column is pushed by the same external atmospheric pressure from all directions. At the height of the hole, the pressure in the water exceeds atmospheric pressure by the value of the hydrostatic pressure. A pressure difference occurs, and water flows out of the hole until the hydrostatic pressure disappears, meaning the water level drops to the level of the hole.

The Law of Communicating Vessels

Experiment

The experiment demonstrates how fluid levels equalize when the fluid is free to flow from one part of the communicating vessels to another. In such cases, equilibrium is established over time, and the pressures in the different parts of the vessel become equal at the same horizontal height. This is the law of communicating vessels.

In the columns of communicating vessels—for a continuous fluid of the same type—the pressures are equal along the same horizontal plane when the fluid is in equilibrium. Consequently, the free fluid surfaces lie at the same height.

Example

Mercury is located at the bottom of an U-tube. Water is carefully poured into the left column of the U-tube, forming a water column with a height of h1=27.2 cmh_1 = 27.2\text{ cm}. The right column remains open. The two liquids do not mix. (The density of water is ρ1=1000 kg/m3\rho_1 = 1000\text{ kg/m}^3, and the density of mercury is ρ2=13,600 kg/m3\rho_2 = 13,600\text{ kg/m}^3.)

  • What is the hydrostatic pressure in the left column at the height of the contact interface between the water and the mercury?
  • Is it true that the pressure is the same at this exact height in the right column? Why?
  • By how much does the mercury level rise in the right column relative to the level of the contact interface on the left (h2h_2)?

Let us calculate the hydrostatic pressure of the water column in the left branch at the level of the contact interface (h1=0.272 mh_1 = 0.272\text{ m}):

phydr=ρ1⋅g⋅h1=1000⋅9.81⋅0.272≈2668.3 Pa p_{\text{hydr}} = \rho_1 \cdot g \cdot h_1 = 1000 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 0.272 \approx 2668.3\text{ Pa}

According to the law of communicating vessels, pressures in an equilibrium system are equal at the same horizontal height, provided we are within the same continuous fluid. If we draw a horizontal plane at the height of the left contact interface, mercury still occupies this level in the right column. Because the two points are connected from below by continuous mercury, the pressure at this level in the right column must exactly match the pressure on the left.

In the right column, in addition to the external atmospheric pressure, the pressure of a mercury column of height h2h_2 loads this level. Since both columns are open at the top, external atmospheric pressure is present on both sides, so the hydrostatic pressures must be equal:

p1=p2 p_1 = p_2

ρ1⋅g⋅h1=ρ2⋅g⋅h2 \rho_1 \cdot g \cdot h_1 = \rho_2 \cdot g \cdot h_2

We can simplify by the acceleration due to gravity (gg):

ρ1⋅h1=ρ2⋅h2 \rho_1 \cdot h_1 = \rho_2 \cdot h_2

From this, we can calculate the height of the mercury column on the right (h2h_2):

h2=ρ1⋅h1ρ2=1000⋅0.27213,600=0.02 m=2 cm h_2 = \frac{\rho_1 \cdot h_1}{\rho_2} = \frac{1000 \cdot 0.272}{13,600} = 0.02\text{ m} = 2\text{ cm}

The mercury level in the right column is therefore exactly 2 cm2\text{ cm} higher than the water-mercury interface on the left.

We can see that because the density of mercury is much higher, a much lower column of it can maintain equilibrium with the taller water column. If, however, we were to choose a higher horizontal plane where water is still present in the left column but air is already present in the right column, the pressures there would no longer be equal, since we would not be in the same fluid.

Measuring Atmospheric Pressure

Experiment

In this experiment, the mercury levels settle at different heights in the dish and in the tube. At the free surface of the mercury in the dish, the pressure is atmospheric pressure, since the mercury is in contact with the air. In the tube, the mercury settles at a height of approximately 76 cm76\text{ cm}. What can be inside the tube above the mercury? This space is practically a vacuum (Torricelli’s vacuum), since the tube is closed and air could not enter. Mercury evaporates to a small extent, so there is mercury vapor of very low pressure above the mercury in the tube, but its pressure is negligible.

In the tube, at the height of the external mercury surface (along a horizontal plane), the pressure matches the atmospheric pressure due to the law of communicating vessels. Therefore, the external atmospheric pressure maintains equilibrium with the hydrostatic pressure of the mercury column inside the tube. This is how Torricelli first measured atmospheric pressure.
