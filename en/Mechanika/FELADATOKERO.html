Exercises on Force I

Examples of the Second Law

  1. A car with a mass of 1300 kg1300\text{ kg} accelerates to a speed of 100.0 kmh100.0\text{ }\frac{\text{km}}{\text{h}} in 8.00 s8.00\text{ s}. What is the reached speed in m/s\text{m/s}? What is the acceleration? Over what distance does the car accelerate? What is the accelerating force in N\text{N}?

v=st=100 km1 h=100000 m3600 s≈27.78 ms v = \frac {s} {t} = \frac {100\text{ km}} {1\text{ h}} = \frac {100\ 000\text{ m}} {3600\text{ s}} \approx 27.78\text{ }\frac {\text{m}} {\text{s}}

Here, to convert the reached speed, we used the basic concept of uniform motion, since we can assume that the car would continue at this constant speed after the acceleration phase. This means it would cover a distance of 100 km100\text{ km} in 1 h1\text{ h}. We know that 1 hour=3600 seconds1\text{ hour} = 3600\text{ seconds}, since every minute is 60 s60\text{ s}.

a=Δvt=v−v0t=27.78−08.00≈3.473 ms2 a = \frac {\Delta v} {t} = \frac {v - v_0} {t} = \frac {27.78 - 0} {8.00} \approx 3.473\text{ }\frac {\text{m}} {\text{s}^2}

s=a2⋅t2=3.4732⋅8.002≈111.1 m s = \frac {a} {2} \cdot t^2 = \frac {3.473} {2} \cdot 8.00^2 \approx 111.1\text{ m}

Fnet=m⋅a=1300⋅3.473≈4515 N≈4.52 kN F_{\text{net}} = m \cdot a = 1300 \cdot 3.473 \approx 4515\text{ N} \approx 4.52\text{ kN}

  1. A car with a mass of 1500 kg1500\text{ kg} travels uniformly at a speed of 50 kmh50\text{ }\frac{\text{km}}{\text{h}}. A pedestrian suddenly steps out in front of the driver, who slams on the brakes. The driver’s reaction time is 0.300 s0.300\text{ s}. The decelerating force acting on the car is 8.83 kN8.83\text{ kN}. Calculate the deceleration in ms2\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}! What is the braking time, and how much time elapses from spotting the pedestrian to coming to a complete stop? How much distance does the car travel during the reaction time and during braking? What is the total stopping distance?

v0=st=50 km1 h=50000 m3600 s≈13.89 ms v_0 = \frac {s} {t} = \frac {50\text{ km}} {1\text{ h}} = \frac {50\ 000\text{ m}} {3600\text{ s}} \approx 13.89\text{ }\frac {\text{m}} {\text{s}}

During the reaction time, the car is still traveling without decelerating:

v0=s1t1 v_0 = \frac {s_1} {t_1}

13.89=s10.3 13.89 = \frac {s_1} {0.3}

s1=13.89⋅0.3≈4.167 m s_1 = 13.89 \cdot 0.3 \approx 4.167\text{ m}

From the magnitude of the braking force, the absolute value of the acceleration is:

Fnet=m⋅|a| F_{\text{net}} = m \cdot |a|

8830=1500⋅|a| 8830 = 1500 \cdot |a|

|a|=88301500≈5.887 ms2 |a| = \frac {8830} {1500} \approx 5.887\text{ }\frac {\text{m}} {\text{s}^2}

Since this is braking, the acceleration value is opposite to the motion, i.e., −5.887 ms2-5.887\text{ }\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}.

a=Δvt=v−v0t a = \frac {\Delta v} {t} = \frac {v - v_0} {t}

−5.887=0−13.89t -5.887 = \frac {0 - 13.89} {t}

t=−13.89−5.887≈2.359 s t = \frac {-13.89} {-5.887} \approx 2.359\text{ s}

The pure braking distance based on the quadratic distance law:

s2=v0⋅t+a2⋅t2=13.89⋅2.359+−5.8872⋅2.3592≈16.39 m s_2 = v_0 \cdot t + \frac {a} {2} \cdot t^2 = 13.89 \cdot 2.359 + \frac {-5.887} {2} \cdot 2.359^2 \approx 16.39\text{ m}

The total duration from spotting to stopping:

ttotal=t1+t=0.3 s+2.359 s≈2.66 s t_{\text{total}} = t_1 + t = 0.3\text{ s} + 2.359\text{ s} \approx 2.66\text{ s}

The total distance traveled (total stopping distance):

stotal=s1+s2=4.167 m+16.39 m≈20.6 m s_{\text{total}} = s_1 + s_2 = 4.167\text{ m} + 16.39\text{ m} \approx 20.6\text{ m}

Therefore, if the pedestrian is within 20.6 m20.6\text{ m} at the moment of spotting, the car has no physical chance of avoiding the collision.

Examples of Weight

  1. A gymnast with a mass of 60.0 kg60.0\text{ kg} lands on a springboard with a vertically downward velocity of 2.00 ms2.00\text{ }\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}, and then bounces off after 0.300 s0.300\text{ s} with a vertically upward velocity of 4.00 ms4.00\text{ }\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}. What is the vertical acceleration of the gymnast? What is the gravitational force acting on the gymnast, and what is their weight at rest? What is the weight of the gymnast during the contact with the springboard, as well as in the air? Calculate how high the gymnast’s center of mass rises after takeoff! The acceleration due to gravity is 9.81 ms29.81\text{ }\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}.

Fg=m⋅g=60⋅9.81=588.6 N F_g = m \cdot g = 60 \cdot 9.81 = 588.6\text{ N}

The gravitational force acting on the gymnast and their weight at rest are both 588.6 N588.6\text{ N}.

Let us choose a coordinate axis pointing upward! In this case, the initial velocity points downward, so it is negative (−2.00 ms-2.00\text{ }\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}), and the final velocity is positive (4.00 ms4.00\text{ }\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}). The uniform acceleration during the bounce is:

a=v−v0t=4.00−(−2.00)0.3=6.000.3=20.00 ms2 a = \frac {v - v_0} {t} = \frac {4.00 - (-2.00)} {0.3} = \frac{6.00}{0.3} = 20.00\text{ }\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}

The gymnast is accelerated upward by the elastic normal force NN of the springboard, while gravity pulls them downward. The magnitude of the net force from Newton’s second law:

Fnet=m⋅a=60.0⋅20.0=1200 N F_{\text{net}} = m \cdot a = 60.0 \cdot 20.0 = 1200\text{ N}

Since the acceleration points upward, the normal force NN is larger:

Fnet=N−Fg F_{\text{net}} = N - F_g

1200=N−588.6 1200 = N - 588.6

N=1200+588.6=1788.6 N N = 1200 + 588.6 = 1788.6\text{ N}

Since the weight of the body equals the normal force (Fw=NF_w = N), the weight of the gymnast during the bounce increases to approximately 1790 N1790\text{ N} due to the sudden upward acceleration. In the air, the body is in a state of weightlessness (Fw=0 NF_w = 0\text{ N}), as only the gravitational force acts on it there. The rising height of the center of mass can be calculated using the equations of vertical motion:

a=v−v0t a = \frac {v - v_0} {t}

−9.81=0−4.00t -9.81 = \frac {0 - 4.00} {t}

t=−4.00−9.81≈0.4077 s t = \frac {-4.00} {-9.81} \approx 0.4077\text{ s}

h=s=v0⋅t+a2⋅t2=4.00⋅0.4077+−9.812⋅0.40772≈0.8155 m h = s = v_0 \cdot t + \frac {a} {2} \cdot t^2 = 4.00 \cdot 0.4077 + \frac {-9.81} {2} \cdot 0.4077^2 \approx 0.8155\text{ m}

In the air, the acceleration due to gravity decelerates the gymnast, which is why we took the value of aa as negative. The body obviously rises until its velocity decreases to zero (v=0 msv = 0\text{ }\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}). Thus, the rising height is approximately 0.816 m0.816\text{ m}.

  1. A body with a mass of 100 g100\text{ g} accelerates vertically downward with an acceleration of 2 ms22\text{ }\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}. The acceleration due to gravity is 9.81 ms29.81\text{ }\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}. What is the gravitational force acting on the body, what is the net force, and with what normal force NN do we support the body vertically upward?

You can verify the obtained answers by running the following simulation:

Weight of a vertically downward accelerating body simulator

Calculating the gravitational force (converting mass to kilograms: 100 g=0.1 kg100\text{ g} = 0.1\text{ kg}):

Fg=m⋅g=0.1⋅9.81=0.981 N F_g = m \cdot g = 0.1 \cdot 9.81 = 0.981\text{ N}

In the inertial reference frame fixed to the Earth, the net force points downward, and its magnitude is:

Fnet=m⋅a=0.1⋅2=0.2 N F_{\text{net}} = m \cdot a = 0.1 \cdot 2 = 0.2\text{ N}

Since the motion and acceleration are directed downward, the gravitational force is greater than the upward normal force NN (Fg>NF_g > N):

Fnet=Fg−N F_{\text{net}} = F_g - N

0.2 N=0.981 N−N 0.2\text{ N} = 0.981\text{ N} - N

N=0.981−0.2=0.781 N N = 0.981 - 0.2 = 0.781\text{ N}

Since the weight of the body equals the normal force (Fw=NF_w = N), the weight of the downward-accelerating body decreases to 0.781 N0.781\text{ N}.
