Exercises on Planetary Motion

Examples for Kepler’s Third Law

  1. The mean Earth–Sun distance is called the Astronomical Unit (AU). We know that the Earth is one astronomical unit away from the Sun, and its orbital period is 1 year. What is the orbital period of the planet Mars if its mean distance from the Sun is 1.524 AU1.524\text{ AU}? The orbital period of the planet Venus is 0.6152 years0.6152\text{ years}. How far is it on average from the Sun?

a3T2=1 \frac{a^3}{T^2} = 1

Here, aa is given in astronomical units and TT in years.

TMars=aMars3=1.5243≈1.881 years=687.0 days T_{\text{Mars}} = \sqrt{a_{\text{Mars}}^3} = \sqrt{1.524^3} \approx 1.881\text{ years} = 687.0\text{ days}

aVenus=TVenus23=0.615223≈0.7233 AU a_{\text{Venus}} = \sqrt[3]{T_{\text{Venus}}^2} = \sqrt[3]{0.6152^2} \approx 0.7233\text{ AU}

  1. Satellites in a geostationary orbit rotate together with the Earth, meaning their orbital period is 1 day. Based on the first cosmic velocity, calculate at what altitude above the surface these telecommunication satellites are located!

a3T2=R3(2πRgR)2 \frac{a^3}{T^2} = \frac{R^3}{\left(\frac{2\pi R}{\sqrt{gR}}\right)^2}

a3T2=gR24π2 \frac{a^3}{T^2} = \frac{gR^2}{4\pi^2}

a3=gR2T24π2=9.81⋅63700002⋅8640024⋅3.14152≈7.527⋅1022 m3 a^3 = \frac{gR^2 T^2}{4\pi^2} = \frac{9.81 \cdot 6\,370\,000^2 \cdot 86\,400^2}{4 \cdot 3.1415^2} \approx 7.527 \cdot 10^{22}\text{ m}^3

a=7.527⋅10223≈42220000 m=42220 km a = \sqrt[3]{7.527 \cdot 10^{22}} \approx 42\,220\,000\text{ m} = 42\,220\text{ km}

The altitude above the surface:

h=a−R=42220−6370=35850 km h = a - R = 42\,220 - 6\,370 = 35\,850\text{ km}

In reality, an altitude of 35786 km35\,786\text{ km} is used, which matches the value obtained from our calculation to 3 significant figures.

  1. The International Space Station (ISS) orbits at an altitude of 420 km420\text{ km} above the Earth’s surface. Calculate its orbital period! What is its velocity at this altitude? What percentage of the first cosmic velocity is this?

a3T2=gR24π2 \frac{a^3}{T^2} = \frac{gR^2}{4\pi^2}

T2=4π2a3gR2=4π2⋅(6370000+420000)39.81⋅63700002 T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2 a^3}{gR^2} = \frac{4\pi^2 \cdot (6\,370\,000 + 420\,000)^3}{9.81 \cdot 6\,370\,000^2}

T≈5572 s≈93 min T \approx 5\,572\text{ s} \approx 93\text{ min}

v=2πaT=2⋅3.1415⋅(6370000+420000)5572≈7656 m/s v = \frac{2\pi a}{T} = \frac{2 \cdot 3.1415 \cdot (6\,370\,000 + 420\,000)}{5\,572} \approx 7\,656\text{ m/s}

76567905⋅100≈96.85% \frac{7\,656}{7\,905} \cdot 100 \approx 96.85\%

This is therefore 96.9%96.9\% of the first cosmic velocity. It is so close because the altitude is relatively small compared to the Earth’s radius, making the orbit very similar to what was assumed when calculating the first cosmic velocity. At this altitude, air resistance is not yet completely zero, which is why a small amount of propulsion is occasionally applied so that the space station does not lose altitude.

Calculating Areal Velocity Based on Orbital Data

In the following, we will need the relationship between areal velocity and orbital (linear) velocity. Let the planet initially be at point AA, and within a very short time interval t≪Tt \ll T, it reaches point BB. The Sun is at point OO. Let rr denote the length of the line segment from the Sun to the planet. Let α\alpha be the angle enclosed by rr and AB¯\overline{AB}, which is no greater than 90∘90^\circ.

Then:

AB¯=vt \overline{AB} = vt

The areal velocity:

AreaOABt=AB¯⋅hAB2t=vt⋅rsin⁡α2t=12vrsin⁡α \frac{\text{Area}_{OAB}}{t} = \frac{\frac{\overline{AB} \cdot h_{AB}}{2}}{t} = \frac{vt \cdot r \sin \alpha}{2t} = \frac{1}{2}vr \sin \alpha

If α=90∘\alpha = 90^\circ, then sin⁡α=1\sin \alpha = 1, meaning that the areal velocity is half the product of the radius and the velocity.

Examples for Kepler’s Second Law

  1. The closest distance of Mars to the Sun along its elliptical orbit (perihelion) is 206.6 million km206.6\text{ million km}, and its farthest point (aphelion) is 249.2 million km249.2\text{ million km}. What is the mean distance of Mars from the Sun, meaning half the length of the major axis? How many astronomical units (AU) is this distance? If the orbital period of Mars is 687.0 days687.0\text{ days}, what is the areal velocity? Use the area formula for an ellipse: Areaellipse=abπ\text{Area}_{\text{ellipse}} = ab\pi! What is the planet’s velocity at the points closest to and farthest from the Sun?

2a=rmin+rmax=206.6⋅106+249.2⋅106=455.8⋅106 km 2a = r_{\text{min}} + r_{\text{max}} = 206.6 \cdot 10^6 + 249.2 \cdot 10^6 = 455.8 \cdot 10^6\text{ km}

a=227.9⋅106 km a = 227.9 \cdot 10^6\text{ km}

a=227.9⋅106 km149.6⋅106 km≈1.523 AU a = \frac{227.9 \cdot 10^6\text{ km}}{149.6 \cdot 10^6\text{ km}} \approx 1.523\text{ AU}

For the areal velocity, we must calculate the semi-minor axis of the ellipse!

c=a−rmin=227.9⋅106−206.6⋅106=21.3⋅106 km c = a - r_{\text{min}} = 227.9 \cdot 10^6 - 206.6 \cdot 10^6 = 21.3 \cdot 10^6\text{ km}

b=a2−c2=(227.9⋅106)2−(21.3⋅106)2≈226.9⋅106 km b = \sqrt{a^2 - c^2} = \sqrt{(227.9 \cdot 10^6)^2 - (21.3 \cdot 10^6)^2} \approx 226.9 \cdot 10^6\text{ km}

Now it is easy to calculate the area of the ellipse:

Areaellipse=abπ=227.9⋅106⋅226.9⋅106⋅3.1415≈162400⋅1012 km2 \text{Area}_{\text{ellipse}} = ab\pi = 227.9 \cdot 10^6 \cdot 226.9 \cdot 10^6 \cdot 3.1415 \approx 162\,400 \cdot 10^{12}\text{ km}^2

Dividing this by the orbital period gives the areal velocity:

AreaellipseT=162400⋅1012687⋅86400≈2736⋅106 km2/s \frac{\text{Area}_{\text{ellipse}}}{T} = \frac{162\,400 \cdot 10^{12}}{687 \cdot 86\,400} \approx 2\,736 \cdot 10^6\text{ km}^2\text{/s}

At the perihelion and aphelion points, the areal velocity is half the product of the radius and the velocity. At these points, the radius is perpendicular to the velocity vector.

12rminvmax=12rmaxvmin=2.736⋅109 km2/s \frac{1}{2}r_{\text{min}} v_{\text{max}} = \frac{1}{2}r_{\text{max}} v_{\text{min}} = 2.736 \cdot 10^9\text{ km}^2\text{/s}

Thus:

vmax=2⋅2.736⋅109206.6⋅106≈26.48 km/s v_{\text{max}} = \frac{2 \cdot 2.736 \cdot 10^9}{206.6 \cdot 10^6} \approx 26.48\text{ km/s}

vmin=2⋅2.736⋅109249.2⋅106≈21.96 km/s v_{\text{min}} = \frac{2 \cdot 2.736 \cdot 10^9}{249.2 \cdot 10^6} \approx 21.96\text{ km/s}

  1. Calculate the same parameters as in the first example, but this time for the Moon. The closest Moon–Earth distance (perigee) is 363300 km363\,300\text{ km}, and the farthest (apogee) is 405500 km405\,500\text{ km}. What is the mean Moon–Earth distance (half of the major axis)? What is the areal velocity if the orbital period is 27.32 days27.32\text{ days}? What are the minimum and maximum velocities? (Use the formula Areaellipse=abπ\text{Area}_{\text{ellipse}} = ab\pi for the area of the ellipse!)

Let MM be the mass of the Earth and mm be the mass of the Moon.

2a=rmin+rmax=363300+405500=768800 km 2a = r_{\text{min}} + r_{\text{max}} = 363\,300 + 405\,500 = 768\,800\text{ km}

a=384400 km a = 384\,400\text{ km}

Calculating the parameters of the ellipse:

c=a−rmin=384400−363300=21100 km c = a - r_{\text{min}} = 384\,400 - 363\,300 = 21\,100\text{ km}

b=a2−c2=3844002−211002≈383820 km b = \sqrt{a^2 - c^2} = \sqrt{384\,400^2 - 21\,100^2} \approx 383\,820\text{ km}

The areal velocity:

abπT=384400⋅383820⋅3.1415927.32⋅86400≈196400 km2/s \frac{ab\pi}{T} = \frac{384\,400 \cdot 383\,820 \cdot 3.14159}{27.32 \cdot 86\,400} \approx 196\,400\text{ km}^2\text{/s}

We know that:

12rminvmax=12rmaxvmin=196400 km2/s \frac{1}{2}r_{\text{min}} v_{\text{max}} = \frac{1}{2}r_{\text{max}} v_{\text{min}} = 196\,400\text{ km}^2\text{/s}

vmax=2⋅196400363300≈1.081 km/s v_{\text{max}} = \frac{2 \cdot 196\,400}{363\,300} \approx 1.081\text{ km/s}

vmin=2⋅196400405500≈0.9687 km/s v_{\text{min}} = \frac{2 \cdot 196\,400}{405\,500} \approx 0.9687\text{ km/s}
