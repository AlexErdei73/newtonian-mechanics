Newton’s Laws

The Law of Inertia (Newton’s First Law)

Every object continues in its state of rest, or of uniform motion in a straight line, unless it is compelled to change that state by forces impressed upon it.

Demonstration

The demonstration video clearly illustrates this law, but in the history of physics, this realization was not always self-evident. According to the ancient philosopher Aristotle, the active influence of another body is continuously required to sustain motion. Based on his observations, a horse-drawn carriage quickly comes to a halt without the pulling action of the horses, whereas it moves forward uniformly in the presence of that pulling action.

Galileo Galilei was the first to discover that objects do not require the intervention of another body to remain in motion, but rather to change their state of motion. The horse-drawn carriage stops after the horses halt because other bodies (the ground and the air) exert a braking effect on it in the form of rolling resistance and air resistance. The pulling action of the horses actually only counterbalances these retarding influences.

The International Space Station is an excellent location for demonstrating this law because, in a microgravity environment, objects float freely. Consequently, friction can be completely eliminated, and the air resistance that occurs at low velocities is also negligible. We observed similarly ideal conditions in earlier experiments where an object pushed on a horizontal air track performed rectilinear uniform motion, and its state of motion changed only when it came into contact with the bumper placed at the end of the track (acting as another body).

Inertial Reference Frames

The law of inertia—and along with it, the rest of the dynamical laws of physics—is valid exclusively in inertial reference frames.

An inertial frame of reference is a reference frame (coordinate system) in which the law of inertia holds true. The laws of physics are valid in inertial reference frames.

Think of a bus that suddenly accelerates, brakes intensely, or takes a sharp turn: the passengers standing inside lose their balance and fall without being pushed by any actual physical body. Since the motion of objects in this system appears to change “without a cause,” an accelerating vehicle cannot be considered an inertial reference frame.

In the NASA demonstration video, Jeff Williams showcases such an instance: when the space station’s rocket engines perform an orbital correction (a so-called re-boost), a heavy camera left drifting freely inside suddenly accelerates toward the screen without any direct external input. In reality, the camera continues to maintain its previous uniform motion (inertia), and we only perceive it as accelerating because the thrusters cause the space station itself to temporarily become a non-inertial reference frame that simply drives into the camera.

In practice, a coordinate system fixed to the Earth’s surface can be considered an inertial frame of reference with satisfactory accuracy for most mechanical problems. However, the Earth’s rotation about its own axis and its orbit around the Sun can be detected with precise experiments, meaning that a terrestrial laboratory is also not a perfect inertial reference frame.

For a long time, it was believed that a coordinate system fixed to the “fixed stars” was the perfect inertial reference frame. Modern astronomy, however, has proven that the stars in our own galaxy, the Milky Way, are also in motion (orbiting the galactic core), so this system does not provide absolute precision either. Today’s most precise scientific inertial reference frame is therefore no longer anchored to the stars, but to galaxies and quasars that are immensely distant from us and can be considered practically entirely stationary.

Newton’s Second Law (The Fundamental Law of Dynamics)

The acceleration of an object is directly proportional to the vector sum of the forces acting on the object, and inversely proportional to the mass of the object.

F→net=m⋅a→ \vec{F}_{\text{net}} = m \cdot \vec{a}

In this relationship, the net resultant force F→net\vec{F}_{\text{net}} is the vector sum of the individual forces acting on the object:

F→net=F→1+F→2+…+F→n \vec{F}_{\text{net}} = \vec{F}_1 + \vec{F}_2 + \dots + \vec{F}_n

Force, like velocity and acceleration, is therefore a vector quantity. The SI unit of force is the Newton (symbol: N\text{N}). A force has a magnitude of 1 N1\text{ N} if it causes an object with a mass of 1 kg1\text{ kg} to move with an acceleration of exactly 1 ms21\text{ }\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}:

1 N=1 kg⋅1 ms2 1\text{ N} = 1\text{ kg} \cdot 1\text{ }\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}

The Law of Action and Reaction (Newton’s Third Law)

To every action there is always opposed an equal reaction; or the mutual actions of two bodies upon each other are always equal and directed to contrary parts.

This law is briefly referred to as the principle of action-reaction. It is crucial to note that these two forces always act on two different bodies, which is why they can never cancel each other out!

A common misconception among students is that a larger or stronger body exerts a greater force on a smaller one. As Mark Vande Hei’s experiment with a basketball on the space station shows: if an astronaut pushes a light ball away from themselves, the magnitudes of the two interacting forces are identically equal. However, because the astronaut’s mass (and thus their inertia) is significantly greater, the acceleration they undergo under the influence of an equal force will be nearly imperceptible compared to the high-velocity launch of the ball (a=Fma = \frac{F}{m}). Conversely, if two astronauts of equal mass push off from one another in space, the action-reaction principle ensures that both fly backward in opposite directions with completely identical accelerations.
