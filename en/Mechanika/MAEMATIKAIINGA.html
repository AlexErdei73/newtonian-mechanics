The Simple Mathematical Pendulum

The Concept of the Simple Pendulum

Simple pendulum: A point-like body that can perform oscillations under the influence of the gravitational force on an inextensible string of fixed length and negligible mass. The other end of the string is fixed in a reference frame attached to the Earth. During the oscillations, the string remains taut.

Experiment

The experiment beautifully shows that for the case of small opening angles, the period of the conical pendulum matches approximately with the period of the simple pendulum. The uniform circular motion of the conical pendulum happens in synchronization with the oscillations of the pendulum, provided that the opening angle of the cone matches the angle of the pendulum’s oscillation and these are started at the same time. According to this, the pendulum performs a simple harmonic motion to a good approximation in the case of small displacements.

For the period, we also obtained the formula that we are going to derive for the oscillations of the pendulum. However, the agreement is true only for the limiting case of small cone angles!

T=2πlcos⁡Θg T = 2\pi \sqrt{ \frac {l \cos \Theta} {g} }

If rr is the radius of revolution and ll is the length of the string, then it is valid that:

cos⁡Θ=l2−r2l=1−(rl)2 \cos \Theta = \frac {\sqrt {l^2 - r^2}} {l} = \sqrt {1 - \left(\frac r l\right)^2}

In the event that r≪lr \ll l,

cos⁡Θ≈1 \cos \Theta \approx 1

therefore we obtain the following formula for the period of the pendulum with small displacement:

T=2πlg T = 2\pi \sqrt{ \frac l g }

The Equation of Motion

Only the gravitational force and the string tension force TT act on the body. The string tension force points in the direction of the string, while the gravitational force points vertically downward. Let us resolve the gravitational force into its components parallel to the string and perpendicular to it, therefore in the tangential direction! The pendulum performs circular motion, but this is not uniform circular motion. For the components in the direction of the string, the following relationship is valid:

T−mgcos⁡ϕ=macp=mv2l=mlω2 T - mg\cos\phi = ma_{cp} = m\frac {v^2} {l} = ml\omega^2

This equation is suitable for determining the force developing in the string. Right now we are not interested in this. The equation interesting for us relates to the tangential components. Let the positive direction of the components be the direction determined by the increase of the displacement angle ϕ\phi! In this case, the tangential force is the following:

Ft=−mgsin⁡ϕ F_t = -mg\sin\phi

The force is negative because it tries to decrease the displacement ϕ\phi, provided that ϕ\phi is positive. According to Newton’s second law:

Ft=mat F_t = ma_t

Therefore

mat=−mgsin⁡ϕ ma_t = -mg\sin\phi

That is to say

at=−gsin⁡ϕ a_t = -g\sin\phi

The signed value of the tangential acceleration can be obtained from the angular acceleration α\alpha as follows:

at=lα a_t = l\alpha

This is true because the radius of the circular motion in our case is ll, the length of the string.

lα=−gsin⁡ϕ l\alpha = -g\sin\phi

Therefore, the final form of the equation of motion is the following:

α=−glsin⁡ϕ \alpha = - \frac g l \sin\phi

For the angle ϕ\phi, we obtained an equation similar to the equation of motion analogous to simple harmonic motion. Instead of acceleration, the angular acceleration appears here, but on the right side of the equation, instead of the angle ϕ\phi, its sine appears. We would expect the appearance of the angle ϕ\phi, because then the equation could be solved with a cosine function for the variation of the unknown angle ϕ\phi over time, therefore the motion in that case would be simple harmonic motion for the angle ϕ\phi! Although this equation can be solved, its solution is possible only with advanced mathematics and is difficult even there, because it leads to the application of special functions. We choose another path.

Limiting Case of Small Angles

Now let the angle ϕ\phi be very small during the entire duration of the oscillation! In this case, the sine function becomes the following:

sin⁡ϕ=xl≈sl=ϕ \sin\phi = \frac {x} {l} \approx \frac {s} {l} = \phi

Here, the essence of our approximation is that we replaced the arc length 2s2s with the corresponding chord of length 2xfx2xfx, which is a straight line segment. This is obviously more accurate the smaller the displacement ϕ\phi is. This means that in this special case, the following approximations are justified:

x≈s x \approx s

sin⁡ϕ≈ϕ \sin \phi \approx \phi

Therefore, the equation of motion of the pendulum in the case of small displacements takes the following form:

α=−glϕ \alpha = -\frac g l \phi

Let us introduce the following notation!

ω2=gl \omega^2 = \frac g l

In this case, the solution of the equation is a periodic oscillation for the angle ϕ\phi. If we release the pendulum from its extreme position without velocity, then this function is the following:

ϕ=ϕmaxcos⁡(ωt) \phi = \phi_{max} \cos(\omega t)

Multiplying this equation by the length ll of the string, we get that:

s=Acos⁡(ωt) s = A\cos(\omega t)

Based on our approximation, the arc length ss can be replaced by the distance xx measured from the vertical axis of symmetry, so:

x=Acos⁡(ωt) x = A\cos(\omega t)

This is, of course, true only if the maximum displacement angle ϕmax\phi_{max} of the pendulum is also very small in radians.

The Period

ω2=gl \omega^2 = \frac g l

ω=2πT \omega = \frac {2\pi} {T}

4π2T2=gl \frac {4\pi^2} {T^2} = \frac g l

Let us take the reciprocal of both sides!

T24π2=lg \frac {T^2} {4\pi^2} = \frac l g

T2=4π2lg T^2 = 4\pi^2 \frac l g

Finally, taking the square root of both sides, we get the following formula:

T=2πlg T = 2\pi \sqrt {\frac l g}

We see that the period does not depend either on the amplitude or on the mass of the oscillating body. However, the independence from the amplitude is true only for the limiting case of small displacements.

Example

Let us calculate the period of a 1 m1\text{ m} long pendulum! This is also usually called a seconds pendulum. What is the reason for this?

T=2πlg=2⋅3.1415⋅19.81=2.0060 s T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac l g} = 2 \cdot 3.1415 \cdot \sqrt {\frac{1}{9.81}} = 2.0060\text{ s}

The half period of this pendulum (therefore until it swings from one extreme position to the opposite extreme position) is approximately 1 second. The error is only about 3 per mille, but gg and thus the error as well depends slightly on the geographical location.
