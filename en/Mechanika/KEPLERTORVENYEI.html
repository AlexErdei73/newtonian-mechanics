Kepler’s Laws

A Brief History of the Discovery

The view accepted by the Church regarding the motion of the planets was the geocentric worldview. Its core tenet was that the Earth is the center of the universe, with the Sun and the other planets orbiting around it. At the time, planetary orbits were believed to be perfect circles, and uniform circular motion was assumed. Because observational data was difficult to explain with this model, the concept of epicycles—circles moving along other circles—was introduced, describing orbits whose centers themselves moved along a circular path in uniform circular motion. In this description, they essentially followed the system of Ptolemy.

Copernicus’s theory introduced the heliocentric, or Sun-centered, system. In this model, the planets—with Earth being one of them—orbited the Sun along circular paths at a uniform speed. Copernicus wisely allowed his work to be published only on his deathbed, fearing persecution by the Church. His followers were indeed persecuted by the Inquisition: Giordano Bruno was burned at the stake, and Galileo Galilei was forced to recant his teachings.

Tycho Brahe, serving as a Danish court astronomer, conducted the most precise astronomical observations of his era. During this time, he tracked the orbit of the planet Mars for over 20 years. These stood as the most accurate pre-telescope (naked-eye) observations of the period. Towards the end of his life, Tycho fell out of favor with the Danish court when a new king came to power. Consequently, he relocated to Prague, where he became the court astronomer to the Emperor, and it was during this time that he took Kepler on as his assistant. Tycho soon fell ill and passed away, and Kepler inherited his observational records.

Kepler was a theorist, an excellent mathematician, and a committed proponent of the heliocentric worldview. He attempted to interpret the orbital data of the planet Mars using Tycho Brahe’s records, which Tycho had previously used to support his own essentially geocentric worldview. Kepler analyzed the data based on the Copernican heliocentric framework, but assuming circular orbits did not lead to complete success. Due to the high precision of Tycho’s data, circular models yielded unacceptably large errors. It was then that he experimented with an elliptical orbit with the Sun resting at one of the focal points. This model fit the measurement data perfectly, leading him to discover the laws of planetary motion.

Kepler’s Laws of Planetary Motion

1. The planets move in elliptical orbits, with the Sun situated at one of the focal points.

2. The radius vector drawn from the Sun to a planet sweeps out equal areas in equal intervals of time.

3. The squares of the orbital periods of the planets are directly proportional to the cubes of their mean distances from the Sun.

An ellipse is a curve that can be easily drawn by pushing two tacks into a board. These points are the focal points (foci). If a string longer than the distance between them is tied to the tacks, the outline of the ellipse can be drawn using a pencil by keeping the string fully taut with the pencil tip as you move it. For any point on an ellipse, the sum of the distances to the two focal points is constant (which in this case is the total length of the string). Of the two axes of symmetry of the ellipse, the line passing through the foci is the major axis. Half of the segment of the major axis enclosed by the ellipse (the semi-major axis) corresponds to the mean distance from the Sun.

A planet does not travel at a uniform speed along its elliptical path: this is described by the second law. According to this law, a planet travels faster when it is closer to the Sun (perihelion) than when it is farther away from the Sun (aphelion). If we look at the line segment connecting the Sun to the planet (the radius vector), it sweeps out equal areas in equal intervals of time. The area swept out per unit time is the areal velocity, which remains constant throughout the motion.
