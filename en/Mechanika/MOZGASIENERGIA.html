Kinetic Energy

Acceleration Work

Let us investigate the acceleration work required to accelerate a body with a mass of mm from an initial velocity of v0v_0 to a final velocity of vv. Let the duration of the acceleration be tt, consider the acceleration to be constant, and denote it by aa.

The accelerating net force acting on the body is:

Fnet=m⋅a=m⋅v−v0t F_{\text{net}} = m \cdot a = m \cdot \frac {v - v_0} {t}

The length of the acceleration phase expressed from the quadratic distance law (substituting the change in velocity for aa):

s=v0⋅t+a2⋅t2=(v0+v−v02)⋅t=2v0+(v−v0)2⋅t s = v_0 \cdot t + \frac {a} {2} \cdot t^2 = \left(v_0 + \frac {v - v_0} {2}\right) \cdot t = \frac {2v_0 + (v - v_0)} {2} \cdot t

s=v0+v2⋅t s = \frac {v_0 + v} {2} \cdot t

The acceleration work performed, as the product of the force and the displacement:

W=Fnet⋅s=m⋅v−v0t⋅v0+v2⋅t=m2⋅(v−v0)⋅(v+v0)=m2⋅(v2−v02) W = F_{\text{net}} \cdot s = m \cdot \frac {v - v_0} {t} \cdot \frac {v_0 + v} {2} \cdot t = \frac {m} {2} \cdot (v - v_0) \cdot (v + v_0) = \frac {m} {2} \cdot (v^2 - v_0^2)

W=m⋅v22−m⋅v022 W = \frac {m \cdot v^2} {2} - \frac {m \cdot v_0^2} {2}

Our result clearly shows that a quantity structured as m⋅v22\frac {m \cdot v^2} {2} is crucial for calculating acceleration work, since the change (difference) in this physical state indicator determines the work done. This quantity is called kinetic energy. The acceleration work is spent on increasing the kinetic energy of the body. Like all forms of energy, its unit is the joule (J).

Kinetic Energy

Kinetic energy is half the product of the body’s mass and the square of its instantaneous velocity. Symbol: EmE_m or EkE_k.

Em=12⋅m⋅v2 E_m = \frac{1}{2} \cdot m \cdot v^2

The Work-Energy Theorem

The acceleration (or deceleration) work done on a body is equal to the change in the kinetic energy of the body.

W=ΔEm=Em−Em,0 W = \Delta E_m = E_m - E_{m,0}

In the derivation above, we proved the work-energy theorem only for the special case of rectilinear, uniformly accelerated motion, but the theorem is generally valid for motions along any path and changing arbitrarily.

Examples

  1. A car with a mass of 1300 kg1300\text{ kg} accelerates from a speed of 10.0 ms10.0\text{ }\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} to a speed of 20.0 ms20.0\text{ }\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}. What is its kinetic energy initially, and what does it increase to by the end of the acceleration? What is the acceleration work performed by the engine, neglecting internal losses? Calculate the work traditionally as well, by determining the acceleration, the accelerating force, and the distance covered! Do the results match?

The initial kinetic energy of the body:

Em,0=m⋅v022=1300⋅1022=65,000 J E_{m,0} = \frac {m \cdot v_0^2} {2} = \frac {1300 \cdot 10^2} {2} = 65,000\text{ J}

The final kinetic energy of the body:

Em=m⋅v22=1300⋅2022=260,000 J E_m = \frac {m \cdot v^2} {2} = \frac {1300 \cdot 20^2} {2} = 260,000\text{ J}

Based on the work-energy theorem, the acceleration work of the engine is the difference between the two energy values:

W=ΔEm=Em−Em,0=260,000−65,000=195,000 J=195.0 kJ W = \Delta {E_m} = E_m - E_{m,0} = 260,000 - 65,000 = 195,000\text{ J} = 195.0\text{ kJ}

Thus, the acceleration work is 195 kJ195\text{ kJ}. Let’s calculate this from the perspective of kinematics and dynamics as well! Let us arbitrarily assume that the duration of the acceleration was exactly 10 s10\text{ s}:

a=v−v0t=20−1010=1 ms2 a = \frac {v - v_0} {t} = \frac {20 - 10} {10} = 1\text{ }\frac {\text{m}} {\text{s}^2}

Fnet=m⋅a=1300⋅1=1300 N F_{\text{net}} = m \cdot a = 1300 \cdot 1 = 1300\text{ N}

The length of the acceleration phase based on the quadratic distance law:

s=v0⋅t+a2⋅t2=10⋅10+12⋅102=100+50=150 m s = v_0 \cdot t + \frac {a} {2} \cdot t^2 = 10 \cdot 10 + \frac {1} {2} \cdot 10^2 = 100 + 50 = 150\text{ m}

According to the definition of mechanical work:

W=Fnet⋅s=1300⋅150=195,000 J=195.0 kJ W = F_{\text{net}} \cdot s = 1300 \cdot 150 = 195,000\text{ J} = 195.0\text{ kJ}

It can be seen that the two different approaches yield exactly the same final result, which proves that using the work-energy theorem can save a lot of unnecessary intermediate calculations.

  1. A stone with a mass of 0.10 kg0.10\text{ kg} is thrown vertically upward with an initial velocity of 5 ms5\text{ }\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}. What is the work done by the gravitational force until the velocity of the stone decelerates to 2 ms2\text{ }\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}? Determine the result without the work-energy theorem, directly from the product of the gravitational force and the displacement as well!

The initial and final kinetic energies:

Em,0=m⋅v022=0.10⋅522=1.25 J E_{m,0} = \frac {m \cdot v_0^2} {2} = \frac {0.10 \cdot 5^2} {2} = 1.25\text{ J}

Em=m⋅v22=0.10⋅222=0.20 J E_m = \frac {m \cdot v^2} {2} = \frac {0.10 \cdot 2^2} {2} = 0.20\text{ J}

Based on the work-energy theorem, the work done by gravity:

W=ΔEm=0.20−1.25=−1.05 J W = \Delta E_m = 0.20 - 1.25 = -1.05\text{ J}

The work of the gravitational force is negative because it opposes the motion (decelerates the stone). Let’s verify this through dynamics! The magnitude of the gravitational force acting on the body (g=9.81 ms2g = 9.81\text{ }\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}):

Fnet=Fg=m⋅g=0.1⋅9.81=0.981 N F_{\text{net}} = F_g = m \cdot g = 0.1 \cdot 9.81 = 0.981\text{ N}

The value of deceleration along the vertical axis is a=−9.81 ms2a = -9.81\text{ }\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}, and the change in velocity is Δv=v−v0=2−5=−3 ms\Delta v = v - v_0 = 2 - 5 = -3\text{ }\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}. The duration of the ascent:

a=Δvt⟹−9.81=−3t⟹t=−3−9.81≈0.3058 s a = \frac {\Delta v} {t} \implies -9.81 = \frac {-3} {t} \implies t = \frac {-3} {-9.81} \approx 0.3058\text{ s}

The vertical displacement (height of ascent) covered during this time:

s=h=v0⋅t+a2⋅t2=5⋅0.3058+−9.812⋅0.30582≈1.529−0.458=1.071 m s = h = v_0 \cdot t + \frac {a} {2} \cdot t^2 = 5 \cdot 0.3058 + \frac {-9.81} {2} \cdot 0.3058^2 \approx 1.529 - 0.458 = 1.071\text{ m}

Since the upward displacement and the downward gravitational force are in opposite directions (α=180∘\alpha = 180^\circ):

W=−Fnet⋅s=−0.981⋅1.071≈−1.050 J W = -F_{\text{net}} \cdot s = -0.981 \cdot 1.071 \approx -1.050\text{ J}

Within the limits of calculation accuracy, the results match perfectly.
