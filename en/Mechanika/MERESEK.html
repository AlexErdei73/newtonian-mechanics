Measurements and Units

A measurement is always the process of determining how many times a selected unit fits into a given physical quantity.

For instance, if we measure the length of a room and find it to be 3 m3\text{ m}, it means that a standardized one-meter reference rod fits exactly three times, back-to-back, along the length of that room.

It is crucial to remember that a physical quantity is always the product of a numerical value and a unit of measurement. For example:

3 m=3⋅1 m 3\text{ m} = 3 \cdot 1\text{ m}

Here, 33 is the numerical value, and m\text{m} is the symbol for the unit of length (the meter). The expression 3 m3\text{ m} is understood to mean that the length in question is exactly three times the length of 1 meter1\text{ meter}.

In classical mechanics, we establish our framework upon three fundamental base quantities. Their definitions, measurements, and standardized units are discussed below.

Fundamental Base Quantities

Length

The SI unit of length is the meter (symbol: m\text{m}). Historically, one meter was defined as exactly the 1/40,000,000-th1/40,000,000\text{-th} part of the Earth’s meridian (longitudinal circumference) passing through Paris. Consequently, the total length of this meridian circle equals exactly 40,000 km40,000\text{ km}.

The kilometer is equal to 1,0001,000 times the meter. The prefix “kilo-” can be attached to other units as well; in all contexts, it represents a multiplier of 1,0001,000. The symbol for the kilo- prefix is k\text{k}, which is written directly before the unit symbol (in this case, m\text{m}). These standard metric prefixes are systematically summarized in the table below.

As a matter of historical interest, the meridian arc formulation represents the very first, revolutionary definition of the meter conceived during the French Enlightenment. Today, modern physics utilizes a far more stable definition. The meter is now defined based on a universal constant of nature: the exact distance light travels in a vacuum during a fraction equal to 1/299,792,458-th1/299,792,458\text{-th} of a second.

Time

It has long been recognized that diverse natural processes can be compared based on the rate at which they unfold. This realization forms the foundation of timekeeping, which was initially executed using sand hourglasses and water clocks. Over centuries, engineers developed increasingly precise mechanical clocks that relied on the steady oscillations of a pendulum or the periodic, resonant motion of a balance wheel assembly. Historically, the base unit of time was anchored to the rotational period of the Earth, a paradigm that persisted until the advent of modern atomic clocks.

The SI unit of time is the second (symbol: s\text{s}). Historically, the second was defined as the 1/86,400-th1/86,400\text{-th} part of a mean solar day (a full rotation of the Earth relative to the Sun); as a result, one full day contains exactly 86,400 s86,400\text{ s}.

This precise numerical constant is derived directly from the standard divisions of a day into 24 hours, an hour into 60 minutes, and a minute into 60 seconds:

1 min=60 s 1\text{ min} = 60\text{ s}

1 h=60 min=60⋅60 s=3600 s 1\text{ h} = 60\text{ min} = 60 \cdot 60\text{ s} = 3600\text{ s}

1 day=24 h=24⋅3600 s=86,400 s 1\text{ day} = 24\text{ h} = 24 \cdot 3600\text{ s} = 86,400\text{ s}

The Seconds Pendulum: A simple mathematical pendulum with a length of approximately 1 m1\text{ m} oscillates small amplitudes with a total period (swing time) of roughly 2 s2\text{ s}. Consequently, the single stroke from one extreme maximum displacement position to the opposite side (half a period) takes almost exactly 1 second1\text{ second}.

However, macroscopic pendulum oscillations are fundamentally unsuited for the rigid definitions required by modern physics. The period of a pendulum exhibits subtle variations depending on its exact geographic location (due to local fluctuations in gravitational acceleration), ambient temperature changes (due to thermal expansion of the rod), and the amplitude of the swing itself.

Mass

The mass of macroscopic objects is compared using balances and scales. Traditional mechanical balances generally operate by counterbalancing the gravitational force—or weight—acting upon the bodies. While the precise physics of weight will be treated thoroughly in upcoming chapters, it is sufficient for now to state that near the Earth’s surface, a mass must be supported or suspended to remain at rest. Under these conditions, the object exerts a force on its support or suspension that is directly proportional to its mass. This primary physical action allows us to quantify mass.

The SI unit of mass is the kilogram (symbol: kg\text{kg}). One kilogram was historically defined as the exact mass represented by one cubic decimeter (1 dm31\text{ dm}^3) of chemically pure, distilled water at its temperature of maximum density (4 ∘C4\text{ }^\circ\text{C}).

This definition links mass directly to volume. The volume of a rectangular prism is the product of its three mutually perpendicular edge lengths. Since a cube is a special case where all edge lengths are identical, its volume is the third power of its edge length. One cubic decimeter represents the volume of a cube with an edge length of 1 dm1\text{ dm}. A decimeter is one-tenth of a meter (10 dm=1 m10\text{ dm} = 1\text{ m}). In everyday nomenclature, the cubic decimeter unit of volume is widely known as the liter (symbol: l\text{l} or L\text{L}).

1 L=1 dm3=1 dm⋅1 dm⋅1 dm=0.1 m⋅0.1 m⋅0.1 m=0.001 m3 1\text{ L} = 1\text{ dm}^3 = 1\text{ dm} \cdot 1\text{ dm} \cdot 1\text{ dm} = 0.1\text{ m} \cdot 0.1\text{ m} \cdot 0.1\text{ m} = 0.001\text{ m}^3

1 m3=1 m⋅1 m⋅1 m=10 dm⋅10 dm⋅10 dm=1000 dm3=1000 L 1\text{ m}^3 = 1\text{ m} \cdot 1\text{ m} \cdot 1\text{ m} = 10\text{ dm} \cdot 10\text{ dm} \cdot 10\text{ dm} = 1000\text{ dm}^3 = 1000\text{ L}

This historical definition explicitly includes temperature because water reaches its maximum density at 4 ∘C4\text{ }^\circ\text{C}; at this specific thermal threshold, a given volume of water exhibits the maximum possible mass.

This complex and difficult-to-reproduce standard was later replaced by the International Prototype of the Kilogram (IPK)—a physical cylinder of platinum-iridium kept in an airtight vault in Sèvres, France. While this eliminated the errors of volume and temperature measurements, it introduced a significant structural flaw: every reference sample worldwide had to be physically compared to a single block of metal. Over long periods, microscopic contamination or wear caused the baseline mass of the physical prototype to drift. To establish absolute long-term stability, the SI system was fundamentally redefined in 2019, locking the definition of the kilogram to a fixed, unalterable constant of the universe: the Planck constant (hh).

Solved Lecture Examples

1. Dimensional Analysis and Scaling: The ground-state radius of a hydrogen atom (the Bohr radius) is precisely 0.053 nm0.053\text{ nm}. Express this dimension in standard meters and picometers. How many Angstroms (Å\text{Å}) does this equal, given that 1 Å=10−10 m1\text{ Å} = 10^{-10}\text{ m}?

  • Solution in Meters: 0.053 nm=0.053⋅10−9 m=5.3⋅10−11 m0.053\text{ nm} = 0.053 \cdot 10^{-9}\text{ m} = 5.3 \cdot 10^{-11}\text{ m}
  • Solution in Picometers: 5.3⋅10−11 m=53⋅10−12 m=53 pm5.3 \cdot 10^{-11}\text{ m} = 53 \cdot 10^{-12}\text{ m} = 53\text{ pm}
  • Solution in Angstroms: 5.3⋅10−11 m=0.53⋅10−10 m=0.53 Å5.3 \cdot 10^{-11}\text{ m} = 0.53 \cdot 10^{-10}\text{ m} = 0.53\text{ Å}

2. Calculating Circumerferences: The mean radius of the Earth is approximately 6371 km6371\text{ km}. Express this radius in meters. Assuming a perfectly spherical Earth geometry, calculate its equatorial circumference in both meters and kilometers.

  • Solution for Radius Conversion: 6371 km=6371⋅103 m=6.371⋅106 m6371\text{ km} = 6371 \cdot 10^{3}\text{ m} = 6.371 \cdot 10^{6}\text{ m}
  • Solution for Circumference: K=2πR=2⋅3.14159⋅6,371,000 m≈40,029,115 m≈40,029 kmK = 2\pi R = 2 \cdot 3.14159 \cdot 6,371,000\text{ m} \approx 40,029,115\text{ m} \approx 40,029\text{ km}

Note: The small discrepancy between this calculated result and a perfect 40,000 km40,000\text{ km} value stems from minute surveying errors during the early French geodetic expeditions. This historical error caused the practical physical meter to be manufactured slightly shorter than its true mathematical blueprint. Furthermore, due to the centrifugal forces generated by its diurnal rotation, the Earth is not a perfect sphere but rather an oblate spheroid, meaning the equatorial radius is larger than the polar radius. Consequently, the actual equatorial circumference is slightly larger, measuring approximately 40,075 km40,075\text{ km}.

  2. The rest mass of an electron is 9.11⋅10−31 kg9.11 \cdot 10^{-31}\text{ kg}. What is the mass of a single electron expressed in grams? What is the mass of one mole of electrons in grams, given that one mole of substance contains exactly 6.022⋅10236.022 \cdot 10^{23} individual electrons?

  3. The speed of light in a vacuum is approximately 300,000 km/s300,000\text{ km/s}. How many meters does light travel in a single second?

  4. A ray of light leaving the Sun takes roughly 8 minutes and 20 seconds8\text{ minutes and } 20\text{ seconds} to reach the Earth. What is the average Sun–Earth distance expressed in kilometers?

  5. A light-year is defined as the distance that light travels in a vacuum during one astronomical year (365.25 days365.25\text{ days}). How many meters and how many kilometers correspond to one light-year?

  6. The closest star to our solar system, Proxima Centauri, is located at a distance of approximately 4.24 light-years4.24\text{ light-years}. How many meters away is this star from the Sun?

  7. The Andromeda Galaxy (the closest major galaxy to our Milky Way) is roughly 2.537⋅106 light-years2.537 \cdot 10^{6}\text{ light-years} away. How many meters and how many kilometers is this immense cosmic distance?

  8. The wavelength of the red light emitted by a helium-neon (He–Ne) gas laser is 632.8 nm632.8\text{ nm}. What is this value expressed in meters and micrometers (μm\mu\text{m})?

  9. According to our current scientific understanding, the radius of the observable universe is approximately 46.5⋅109 light-years46.5 \cdot 10^{9}\text{ light-years}. How many kilometers does this distance equal?

  10. The most recent measured value for the charge radius of a proton is approximately 0.84 fm0.84\text{ fm}. What is this subatomic distance expressed in meters and millimeters?
