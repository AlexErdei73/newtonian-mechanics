Perfectly Elastic, Oblique Collisions

We will now examine some of the more important perfectly elastic collisions that cannot be considered central (head-on) collisions.

Perfectly Elastic Collision with a Massive Body

We have seen that in the central case, a perfectly elastic collision leads to a rebound with the same speed as the impact speed. Now we are interested in the relation between the angle of reflection and the angle of incidence in an oblique collision.

Let us assume that the surface of the massive target is a flat plane, and the smaller mass collides with it. At the point of impact, we draw a line perpendicular to the plane of the massive body; this is the normal line (or normal to the surface). The angle between this normal line and the initial velocity vector is the angle of incidence (α\alpha). The angle of reflection (β\beta) is the angle between the normal line and the velocity vector after the collision.

Simulation

Perfectly elastic, oblique collision with a massive target

As seen in the simulation, the angle of incidence and the angle of reflection are equal. Why is this so? We know two things: 1. Mechanical energy is conserved during the collision since it is a perfectly elastic collision. 2. The massive body exerts a force on the colliding body perpendicular to its surface (friction is neglected).

Let the xx-axis of the coordinate system be perpendicular to the wall and the yy-axis be parallel to it. In this case, the conservation of energy is:

m⋅v022=m⋅v22 \frac{m \cdot v_0^2}{2} = \frac{m \cdot v^2}{2}

v0=v v_0 = v

In other words, based on the conservation of energy, the magnitude of the velocity before the collision is equal to the magnitude of the velocity after the collision. Since there is no force parallel to the wall (Fy=0F_y = 0 because there is no friction), the yy-component of momentum is conserved:

Fnet,y=0→Δpy=0 F_{\text{net},y} = 0 \quad \rightarrow \quad \Delta p_y = 0

m⋅v0,y=m⋅vy m \cdot v_{0,y} = m \cdot v_y

Therefore, the momentum component parallel to the wall remains constant during the collision. If the angle of incidence is α\alpha and the angle of reflection is β\beta (measured from the normal):

v0,y=v0⋅sin⁡α v_{0,y} = v_0 \cdot \sin \alpha

vy=v⋅sin⁡β v_y = v \cdot \sin \beta

Since v0=vv_0 = v and v0,y=vyv_{0,y} = v_y:

m⋅v0⋅sin⁡α=m⋅v⋅sin⁡β m \cdot v_0 \cdot \sin \alpha = m \cdot v \cdot \sin \beta

sin⁡α=sin⁡β \sin \alpha = \sin \beta

α=β \alpha = \beta

Here, we took advantage of the fact that the magnitude of the velocity does not change, so both sides of the equation can be divided by it.

Example

A ball of mass 0.100kg0.100\ \text{kg} strikes a wall with a velocity magnitude of 3.00ms3.00\ \frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} in a perfectly elastic collision and rebounds. The angle of incidence is 30∘30^\circ, and the collision lasts for 1.00ms1.00\ \text{ms}. What is the average force exerted on the wall during the collision?

The component of velocity perpendicular to the wall (in the xx direction) is:

p0,x=m⋅v0,x=m⋅v0⋅cos⁡α=0.100⋅3.00⋅cos⁡30∘≈0.2598kg⋅ms p_{0,x} = m \cdot v_{0,x} = m \cdot v_0 \cdot \cos \alpha = 0.100 \cdot 3.00 \cdot \cos 30^\circ \approx 0.2598\ \frac{\text{kg} \cdot \text{m}}{\text{s}}

Since the collision is elastic, the xx-component of the rebound velocity has the opposite direction:

vx=−v0,x v_x = -v_{0,x}

m⋅vx=−m⋅v0,x m \cdot v_x = -m \cdot v_{0,x}

The change in momentum in the xx direction is:

Δp0,x=px−p0,x=−p0,x−p0,x=−2⋅0.2598=−0.5196kg⋅ms \Delta p_{0,x} = p_x - p_{0,x} = -p_{0,x} - p_{0,x} = -2 \cdot 0.2598 = -0.5196\ \frac{\text{kg} \cdot \text{m}}{\text{s}}

The average force (acting on the ball) is:

Fnet,x=Δpxt=−0.51960.001=−519.6N F_{\text{net},x} = \frac{\Delta p_x}{t} = \frac{-0.5196}{0.001} = -519.6\ \text{N}

The force acting on the wall is the counterforce (reaction force) of this (Newton’s third law):

N=−Fnet,x=519.6N N = -F_{\text{net},x} = 519.6\ \text{N}

Perfectly Elastic Collision of Objects of Equal Mass when the Target is at Rest

Simulation

Perfectly elastic collision of objects of equal mass when the target is at rest

The velocities are perpendicular to each other after the collision, as can be seen from the simulation! Why is this the case?

We know two things: 1. Total momentum is conserved during the collision. 2. Mechanical energy is conserved during the collision.

Conservation of momentum in vector form:

m⋅v→0=m⋅v→1+m⋅v→2 m \cdot \vec{v}_0 = m \cdot \vec{v}_1 + m \cdot \vec{v}_2

Simplifying by mass:

v→0=v→1+v→2 \vec{v}_0 = \vec{v}_1 + \vec{v}_2

The initial velocity is therefore the vector sum of the two final velocities. This means that v→1\vec{v}_1, v→2\vec{v}_2, and v→0\vec{v}_0 form the sides of a triangle, where the longest side is v→0\vec{v}_0.

Due to the conservation of energy:

m⋅v022=m⋅v122+m⋅v222 \frac{m \cdot v_0^2}{2} = \frac{m \cdot v_1^2}{2} + \frac{m \cdot v_2^2}{2}

v02=v12+v22 v_0^2 = v_1^2 + v_2^2

The last equation is none other than the Pythagorean theorem applied to this triangle. This means that the triangle is a right-angled triangle; therefore, v→1\vec{v}_1 and v→2\vec{v}_2 are perpendicular to each other (provided that the collision is not central).

Example

A ball of mass 0.100kg0.100\ \text{kg} moving at a velocity of 5.00ms5.00\ \frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} undergoes a perfectly elastic oblique collision with a stationary ball of identical mass, deflecting with a velocity of 4.00ms4.00\ \frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}. What will be the velocity of the initially stationary ball after the collision? The rotation of the balls is negligible, so they can be considered as point masses. What is the average force between the balls if the collision lasts for 1.00ms1.00\ \text{ms}?

Let us apply the Pythagorean theorem to the velocities:

v02=v12+v22 v_0^2 = v_1^2 + v_2^2

52=42+v22 5^2 = 4^2 + v_2^2

v2=52−42=25−16=9=3ms v_2 = \sqrt{5^2 - 4^2} = \sqrt{25 - 16} = \sqrt{9} = 3\ \frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}

Calculating the force (from the momentum change of the second ball):

Fnet=Δp2t=m⋅v2−0t F_{\text{net}} = \frac{\Delta p_2}{t} = \frac{m \cdot v_2 - 0}{t}

Fnet=0.1⋅30.001=0.30.001=300N F_{\text{net}} = \frac{0.1 \cdot 3}{0.001} = \frac{0.3}{0.001} = 300\ \text{N}

Thus, an average force of 300.0N300.0\ \text{N} arises between the balls during the 1ms1\ \text{ms} collision time. This force accelerates the initially stationary ball to a velocity of 3.00ms3.00\ \frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}.
