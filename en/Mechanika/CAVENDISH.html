The Cavendish Experiment

Significance of the Measurement

The gravitational constant GG present in the law of gravitation was a completely unknown quantity in Newton’s time. Its measurement was achieved by Henry Cavendish approximately 100 years after Newton’s death. It cannot be determined solely from the orbital data of bodies orbiting the Earth because in those equations it always appears multiplied by the mass of the Earth. However, once this constant is measured, we can immediately calculate the mass of the Earth or any other celestial body, including the Sun. Significance of the Measurement

The gravitational constant GG present in the law of gravitation was a completely unknown quantity in Newton’s time. Its measurement was achieved by Henry Cavendish approximately 100 years after Newton’s death. It cannot be determined solely from the orbital data of bodies orbiting the Earth because in those equations it always appears multiplied by the mass of the Earth. However, once this constant is measured, we can immediately calculate the mass of the Earth or any other celestial body, including the Sun. Cavendish thus referred to his measurement as “weighing the Earth.”

The challenge is that the gravitational attraction between normal-sized objects represents an extremely small force. Measuring such a tiny force is very difficult. Even today, our measurements of GG are only accurate to about 4 significant figures, even when using the most sophisticated methods.

Cavendish used a device called a torsion balance, which consists of a dumbbell-shaped body suspended by a thin torsion wire. Two spheres of equal mass and size are located at the ends of the dumbbell, which is suspended at its center. The structure must be isolated as much as possible from all disturbing external influences. If two large lead spheres are placed near the two smaller spheres so that their gravitational attraction twists the torsion wire, the angle of twist can be measured. The gravitational constant can then be calculated using the experimental masses, distances, and the properties of the torsion wire.

Examples

  1. Determine the mass of the Earth, knowing that the acceleration due to gravity is 9.81 m/s29.81\text{ m/s}^2 and the radius of the Earth is 6370 km6370\text{ km}! The measured value of GG is 6.674⋅10−11 N⋅m2/kg26.674 \cdot 10^{-11}\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}^2\text{/kg}^2.

Fg=mg F_{\text{g}} = mg

Fg=GmMER2 F_{\text{g}} = G\frac{mM_{\text{E}}}{R^2}

Here, we have written the gravitational force acting on the body in two different forms! One form is the standard one containing the acceleration due to gravity. The other represents Newton’s law of gravitation applied to this problem.

mg=GmMER2 mg = G\frac{mM_{\text{E}}}{R^2}

g=GMER2 g = \frac{GM_{\text{E}}}{R^2}

From this, we can express the value of MEM_{\text{E}}, the mass of the Earth:

ME=gR2G=9.81⋅637000026.674⋅10−11≈5.964⋅1024 kg M_{\text{E}} = \frac{gR^2}{G} = \frac{9.81 \cdot 6\,370\,000^2}{6.674 \cdot 10^{-11}} \approx 5.964 \cdot 10^{24}\text{ kg}

  1. Determine the mass of the Sun based on the Earth’s orbital data!

Fg=mv2r=m4π2r2T2r F_{\text{g}} = m\frac{v^2}{r} = m\frac{4\pi^2 r^2}{T^2 r}

Fg=GmMSr2 F_{\text{g}} = G\frac{mM_{\text{S}}}{r^2}

Equating the two expressions gives the following:

m4π2rT2=GmMSr2 m\frac{4\pi^2 r}{T^2} = G\frac{mM_{\text{S}}}{r^2}

Here we can divide by mm and express the value of MSM_{\text{S}}:

MS=4π2r3GT2=4⋅3.14152⋅(1.496⋅1011)36.674⋅10−11⋅(365.26⋅86400)2≈1.988⋅1030 kg M_{\text{S}} = \frac{4\pi^2 r^3}{GT^2} = \frac{4 \cdot 3.1415^2 \cdot (1.496 \cdot 10^{11})^3}{6.674 \cdot 10^{-11} \cdot (365.26 \cdot 86\,400)^2} \approx 1.988 \cdot 10^{30}\text{ kg}

  2. Two ocean liners, each with a mass of 50000 tonnes50\,000\text{ tonnes}, are parked next to each other in a harbor. The distance between their centers of mass is 100 m100\text{ m}. How large is the gravitational attractive force acting between them? Compare this force to the weight of a 10 dkg10\text{ dkg} apple!
