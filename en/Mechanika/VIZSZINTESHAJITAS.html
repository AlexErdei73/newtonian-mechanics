Horizontal Projectile Motion *

Demonstration

Simulation

Horizontal Projectile Motion Interactive Simulator

Both the interactive simulation and the experimental demonstration beautifully illustrate that the vertical motion of an object released from rest (free fall) occurs in perfect synchrony with the vertical component of motion of a horizontally projected object. If two objects are launched from the exact same height at precisely the same time, their vertical positions (heights) match identically at every single instant throughout the entire duration of the motion.

Formulas for Horizontal Projectile Motion

Let us project an object with a horizontally directed initial velocity v0v_0 from the origin of a coordinate system (x0=0x_0 = 0, y0=0y_0 = 0). Let us assume that air resistance is negligible and the value of gravitational acceleration is g=9.81 ms2g = 9.81\text{ }\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}. Let the horizontal xx-axis point in the direction of the initial velocity (from left to right), and this time, let the vertical yy-axis point straight downward.

The components of the gravitational acceleration vector (since acceleration points vertically downward and our yy-axis is also directed downward) are:

a→=(ax,ay)=(0,g) \vec{a} = (a_x,\ a_y) = (0,\ g)

The components of the initial velocity vector (since the initial velocity is purely horizontal) are:

v→0=(v0x,v0y)=(v0,0) \vec{v}_0 = (v_{0x},\ v_{0y}) = (v_0,\ 0)

Let us recall the quadratic law of motion for uniformly changing motion in its general form:

s=v0⋅t+a2⋅t2 s = v_0 \cdot t + \frac {a} {2} \cdot t^2

As we have learned previously, when treating composite motions, the corresponding displacement coordinate replaces the distance traveled (ss), and the targeted directional signed coordinates (components) replace v0v_0 and aa.

Horizontal Motion

x−x0=v0x⋅t+ax2⋅t2 x - x_0 = v_{0x} \cdot t + \frac {a_x} {2} \cdot t^2

x−0=v0⋅t+02⋅t2 x - 0 = v_0 \cdot t + \frac {0} {2} \cdot t^2

x=v0⋅t x = v_0 \cdot t

Thus, the object undergoes no acceleration in the horizontal direction, meaning it performs rectilinear uniform motion along the xx-axis.

Vertical Motion

y−y0=v0y⋅t+ay2⋅t2 y - y_0 = v_{0y} \cdot t + \frac {a_y} {2} \cdot t^2

y−0=0⋅t+g2⋅t2 y - 0 = 0 \cdot t + \frac {g} {2} \cdot t^2

y=g2⋅t2 y = \frac {g} {2} \cdot t^2

We can see that the time dependence of the yy-coordinate matches identically with the distance traveled by an object released from rest in free fall. If the time duration tt and the value of gravitational acceleration gg are identical, then the vertical displacement of the two objects is also identical (y=sy = s). This independence and synchrony are what we observed in both the experiment and the simulation.

Examples

  1. From the edge of a 20.0 m20.0\text{ m} high vertical cliff, a stone is projected horizontally with an initial velocity of 10.0 ms10.0\text{ }\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}. After how much time does the stone impact the ground? How far does it travel horizontally, measured from the edge of the cliff? Calculate the magnitude of the stone’s instantaneous velocity at the moment of impact!

The vertical distance covered until impact is y=20.0 my = 20.0\text{ m}. Let us determine the fall time, denoting the unknown by xx:

y=g2⋅t2 y = \frac {g} {2} \cdot t^2

20.0=9.812⋅x2 20.0 = \frac {9.81} {2} \cdot x^2

20.0=4.905⋅x2 20.0 = 4.905 \cdot x^2

x=±2.019 x = \pm 2.019

We reject the negative time value as physically uninterpretable, so impact occurs 2.02 s2.02\text{ s} after launch.

Let us calculate the horizontal distance covered by the stone (the projectile range):

x=v0⋅t=10.0⋅2.019≈20.2 m x = v_0 \cdot t = 10.0 \cdot 2.019 \approx 20.2\text{ m}

The stone flies a horizontal distance of 20.2 m20.2\text{ m} from the cliff wall.

Since the horizontal acceleration component is zero (ax=0a_x = 0), the horizontal component of velocity remains constant throughout the motion:

vx=v0=10.0 ms v_x = v_0 = 10.0\text{ }\frac {\text{m}} {\text{s}}

Let us determine the vertical component of velocity (vyv_y) at the moment of impact, based on the definition of acceleration (denoting the unknown by xx):

ay=vy−v0yt a_y = \frac {v_y - v_{0y}} {t}

9.81=x−02.019 9.81 = \frac {x - 0} {2.019}

x=9.81⋅2.019≈19.81 x = 9.81 \cdot 2.019 \approx 19.81

Thus, vy≈19.81 msv_y \approx 19.81\text{ }\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}.

The pure magnitude (length) of the impact velocity vector can be obtained from its perpendicular components using the Pythagorean theorem:

v=vx2+vy2=10.02+19.812=100+392.44≈22.2 ms v = \sqrt {v_x^2 + v_y^2} = \sqrt {10.0^2 + 19.81^2} = \sqrt{100 + 392.44} \approx 22.2\text{ }\frac {\text{m}} {\text{s}}

  1. The initial velocity of a projectile leaving the barrel of a rifle is 600 ms600\text{ }\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}. If the weapon is fired perfectly horizontally, how much does the bullet drop vertically by the time it hits a target placed at a distance of 150 m150\text{ m}? Air resistance is negligible, and the value of gravitational acceleration is 9.81 ms29.81\text{ }\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}.

Let us first determine the flight time of the projectile from the horizontal, uniform phase of motion (denoting the unknown by xx):

x=v0⋅t x = v_0 \cdot t

150=600⋅x 150 = 600 \cdot x

x=150600=0.250 x = \frac{150}{600} = 0.250

The flight time of the bullet is exactly t=0.250 st = 0.250\text{ s}.

During this time, the drop height corresponding to vertical free fall is:

y=g2⋅t2=9.812⋅0.2502=4.905⋅0.0625≈0.307 m y = \frac {g} {2} \cdot t^2 = \frac {9.81} {2} \cdot 0.250^2 = 4.905 \cdot 0.0625 \approx 0.307\text{ m}

The projectile drops approximately 30.7 cm30.7\text{ cm} along its trajectory, meaning that for a guaranteed hit, the barrel of the weapon must be aimed precisely this much above the center of the target.
