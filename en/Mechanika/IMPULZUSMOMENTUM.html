Angular Momentum

Angular Momentum for a System of Particles

The angular momentum of a point mass is derived from its linear momentum in a manner similar to how torque is derived from a force. Let the axis of rotation be located at the origin, and let this be the zz-axis of the coordinate system. Then, the angular momentum about this axis is calculated as follows:

Lz=mvrsin⁡Θ L_z = mvr \sin \Theta

In this equation, everything refers to the projection of the motion onto the xx-yy plane, so vv is the velocity of the point mass of mass mm in the xx-yy plane, and rr is the distance of the point mass from the axis of rotation. The angle Θ\Theta is the angle (no greater than 180∘180^\circ) enclosed by rr and vv in the xx-yy plane. The sign of the angle is chosen such that a counter-clockwise rotation yields a positive angular momentum, while a clockwise rotation yields a negative angular momentum.

For a system of particles, the angular momentum is the sum of the angular momenta of the individual point masses belonging to the system; therefore, the addition must be performed on these signed quantities, taking their signs into account:

Lz=∑i=1Nmivirisin⁡Θi L_z = \sum_{i = 1}^{N} m_i v_i r_i \sin \Theta_i

Although angular momentum is a vector quantity, we are currently only concerned with its component along the axis of rotation (the zz-direction), which is a signed quantity.

Angular Momentum of a Rigid Body

Let us now consider a rigid body undergoing planar motion in the xx-yy plane about the zz-axis passing through the origin. Since the body is rigid, the distance rir_i for each individual point mass is constant, and the particles undergo circular motion about the axis. In circular motion, the velocity is tangential, which means it is perpendicular to the radius vector. Therefore:

sin⁡Θi=sin⁡90∘=1 \sin \Theta_i = \sin 90^\circ = 1

for every point mass. Thus, the angular momentum can be calculated as follows:

Lz=∑i=1Nrimivi L_z = \sum_{i = 1}^{N} r_i m_i v_i

This means that for every point, we sum the product of its distance from the axis (the radius of the circle) and its linear momentum. The linear momentum here is signed because the velocities viv_i are signed quantities, since they can be written as the product of the radius and the angular velocity, and the angular velocity is a signed quantity.

rimivi=rimiriω=miri2ω r_i m_i v_i = r_i m_i r_i \omega = m_i r_i^2 \omega

Thus, the angular momentum can be written in the following manner:

Lz=(∑i=1Nmiri2)ω=Iω L_z = \left( \sum_{i = 1}^{N} m_i r_i^2 \right) \omega = I \omega

Since the angular velocity is identical for every point, we factored it out of the sum.

Example

Two identical masses of 0.200 kg0.200\text{ kg}, fixed symmetrically to a rigid rod of negligible mass, rotate at an identical distance of 0.300 m0.300\text{ m} from the axis of rotation at a speed of 3.00 m/s3.00\text{ m/s}. The rotation is counter-clockwise. Calculate the angular momentum of the system with respect to the axis of rotation!

Lz=∑i=1Nmiviri=2⋅0.2⋅3⋅0.3=0.360 kg⋅m2/s L_z = \sum_{i = 1}^{N} m_i v_i r_i = 2 \cdot 0.2 \cdot 3 \cdot 0.3 = 0.360\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}^2\text{/s}

The Moment of Inertia

Here, II is called the moment of inertia, and its definition is:

I=∑i=1Nmiri2 I = \sum_{i = 1}^{N} m_i r_i^2

We must sum the product of the mass and the square of the distance from the axis of rotation for all bodies. Thus, the unit of the moment of inertia is kg⋅m2\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^2. We will see that II is the rotational equivalent of mass, meaning it acts to oppose any acceleration or deceleration of the body’s rotation about a given axis.
